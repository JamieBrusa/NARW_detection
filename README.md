## Abstract

Accurately monitoring population metrics relies on minimizing observation biases, such as those associated with detection probability. Many statistical approaches can adequately account for variation in detection probability across individuals in a population. However, in small populations, the behaviors of individuals take on proportionally greater meaning. Therefore, identifying behavioral and methodological drivers of individual detection heterogeneity can lead to improved population monitoring and conservation outlooks. We explored potential drivers of individual detection heterogeneity in North Atlantic right whales (Eubalaena glacialis), an endangered species, using hierarchical logistic regression models, robust design models, and graphical analyses. We found that detection rates varied minimally across individuals observed from ship-based surveys, but detection rates from aerial surveys varied strongly across individuals. From aerial survey model results, we found group size, surface-active group behavior, and geographic zone to have a notable relationship with individual variation in detection rate. We also found substantial variation across individuals in temporary emigration from commonly surveyed geographic zones along the coast from Newfoundland, Canada through Florida, USA, especially Cape Cod Bay. The Gulf of St. Lawrence had the lowest overall temporary emigration rates, and the southeast USA had the highest. Results from our exploratory analyses can inform future population monitoring of this endangered species.
